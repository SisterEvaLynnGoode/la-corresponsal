El taller de carteles — Teacher Guide

Propaganda unit · ten meetings · student packet is 16 pages

Students decode posters that argue both sides of one labour dispute, name nine techniques, then design a poster for a cause they choose. The unit ends with the counter-poster: the slogan their opponents would print, using a technique they just learned. The example posters are drawn originals, not reproductions, so the packet copies forever — grayscale on paper, colour on the projector.

Teaching notes

Teaching notes

Why labour and not war

The duelling pair is a bakers' strike: the union's poster and the company's poster, same week, same fist, same word «nosotros». Students see one technique argue both sides before anyone asks them to pick a side, and the heat stays on who paid the printer rather than on anybody's identity. The ad-versus-public-health pair on day 3 makes the same point with lower stakes: a poster you approve of and a poster you don't, running the same play.

The question that runs the unit

«¿Quién pagó este cartel, y qué quiere que yo haga?» Ask it every single day, about every poster, including the one on the student's own desk on day 9.

What to watch for

  • Posters that are flyers. The commonest failure is four sentences and a border. The three-second test on day 8 fixes it better than you can by telling them.
  • A cause with no action. "Stop racism" is a belief, not a call. Push for the thing a reader does this week: sign, ask, show up, turn it off, bring one.
  • Students who can't draw. One symbol, big, is the whole requirement, and the model poster on page 11 is a light bulb. Lettering counts as art.
  • The counter-poster going soft. If a student writes a weak slogan for the other side, they have dodged the point. Make them write the one that would actually hurt.

Grading

Fifteen points, same table the student uses on page 16. Spelling is not on it — accents on the slogan are coached on page 10 as the shop's house style, the same way the newspaper unit handled them.
